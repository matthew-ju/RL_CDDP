# 5. Stakes Estimator Calibration: Why Training Distribution Determines Boundary Relaxation Quality

A natural question raised by the CDDP architecture is whether the choice of training distribution for the stakes estimator $\mathcal{S}(s;\,\pi_\theta)$ is consequential, or whether any reasonable rollout source would produce an adequate boundary relaxation threshold. We address this question directly by training an alternative critic $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ on rollouts of the unconstrained policy $\pi_u$ — the policy CDDP tends toward under high stakes — and comparing its calibration and behavioral consequences against the strictly-bounded critic $\mathcal{S}_d$ used in the main system. The results reveal that the training distribution is not a secondary implementation detail but a primary determinant of calibration correctness.

---

## 5.1 Score Gap in the Dilemma Zone

We first establish that the two critics produce systematically different scores in the states where the boundary relaxation decision is most consequential. For each state $s$ in the dilemma zone — defined as feasibility margin $T_{\mathrm{rem}} - L_{\mathrm{safe}}(s) \in [-1, +1]$, where strict constraint enforcement is on the boundary of mission feasibility — we query both critics at the same input and record the gap.

Across 851 dilemma-zone observations pooled from both grids, the strictly-bounded critic assigns mean score $\bar{\mathcal{S}}_d = 0.687$, while the PPO-trained critic assigns $\bar{\mathcal{S}}_\theta = 0.355$, a gap of $0.332$ ($p < 10^{-119}$, two-sample $t$-test). Physically, $\mathcal{S}_d$ correctly identifies these states as high-urgency: the strictly-bounded policy is moments from exhausting its feasibility budget, and the critic has learned to signal this. $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ treats the same states as moderately safe, because the rollouts it was trained on — generated by $\pi_u$, which bypasses safe-path constraints — do not represent the time pressure an agent under strict constraint enforcement would face there.

---

## 5.2 Mechanistic Explanation: The Shortcut Effect

The score gap has a specific causal structure that we expose through a controlled rollout experiment. We sample 200 dilemma-zone starting states $s_0$ per grid (feasibility margin $\in [-1, +1]$, RNG seed 42) and execute both $\pi_d$ and $\pi_u$ from the **same** set of states, recording episode length, constraint violations, and the $T_{\mathrm{rem}}$ values encountered during each rollout.

**Definition 6 (Strict Detour Penalty).**
For a starting state $s_0$ in the dilemma zone, define the *strict detour penalty* as the expected additional steps incurred by strict constraint compliance relative to the unconstrained optimal path:

$$
\Delta(s_0) \;=\; \mathbb{E}[\text{steps}(s_0, \pi_d)] - \mathbb{E}[\text{steps}(s_0, \pi_u)],
$$

where $\text{steps}(s_0, \pi)$ denotes episode length when the goal is reached. If the episode ends in failure or timeout, $\text{steps}$ evaluates to the maximum episode step limit. Expectation is over stochastic transitions.

The results confirm a substantial strict detour penalty in both environments. On Grid 1, $\pi_u$ reaches the goal in 3.9 steps on average versus 5.5 steps for $\pi_d$ — a speedup of 1.6 steps (28.7%) achieved at a cost of 1.93 constraint violations per episode, compared to 0.00 for $\pi_d$. On Grid 2, the speedup is 2.3 steps (24.3%) with 1.79 violations per episode for $\pi_u$ versus zero for $\pi_d$. The step-count distributions are visually non-overlapping (Figure~X, panels a--b): $\pi_u$'s distribution is concentrated in short episodes (3--4 steps on Grid 1, 6--8 steps on Grid 2), while $\pi_d$'s distribution is shifted 1--3 steps right and has a heavier tail corresponding to the additional path length required by safe detours.

**Remark 3 (Input Distribution Bias).**
Because $\pi_u$ completes episodes faster, its rollouts generate training observations paired with higher residual $T_{\mathrm{rem}}$ values. Concretely, $\pi_d$ rollouts from dilemma-zone starts produce 76.6% of critic training observations in the low-$T_{\mathrm{rem}}$ range (bins 0--6), compared to 69.3% for $\pi_u$ rollouts — a 7.3 percentage-point difference. More importantly, when $\pi_u$ encounters a dilemma-zone state with low $T_{\mathrm{rem}}$, it typically *succeeds* by violating constraints, so the critic trained on those rollouts learns the association $(\text{dilemma state}, \text{low } T_{\mathrm{rem}}) \to \text{mission success} \to \text{low urgency}$. The strictly-bounded critic sees the same state-timer pair end in constraint-compliant failure, learning the correct association $(\text{dilemma state}, \text{low } T_{\mathrm{rem}}) \to \text{mission failure} \to \text{high urgency}$.

Note that this paired gap of 0.198 is smaller than the 0.332 gap reported in Section 5.1. This is expected: Section 5.1 pools observations from full trajectories (which are biased toward states that each policy visits frequently), whereas this experiment controls for state-visitation bias by fixing identical starting states for both policies. The remaining 0.198 reflects the pure score offset attributable to the shortcut effect, isolated from the confound of differential state coverage.

The causal consequence of this input bias is measured directly: evaluated on 400 **identical** dilemma-zone starting states, $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ scores $0.198$ below $\mathcal{S}_d$ on average ($p = 2.01 \times 10^{-25}$, paired $t$-test, controlling for state identity). In 27.5% of shared states ($N = 110$), $\mathcal{S}_d \geq \tau$ while $\mathcal{S}_\theta < \tau$ — states where $\mathcal{S}_d$ correctly begins to decay the safety penalty and $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ does not. These missed boundary relaxations demonstrate the critic's fundamental blindness to the specific failure modes of strict constraint compliance.

---

## 5.3 Behavioral Consequences of Miscalibration

The score gap is not confined to the dilemma zone. We find that $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ is miscalibrated in both directions depending on the feasibility regime, producing qualitatively different failure modes above and below the dilemma boundary.

**Tight regime** (feasibility margin $\in [-1, +1]$): The unconstrained critic ($\mathcal{S}_\theta$) maintains high task success exclusively by cheating. Its massive False Positive Rate (FPR) causes it to hallucinate danger and drop boundaries prematurely across the state space, allowing the downstream agent to devolve into unconstrained behavior and incur a catastrophic spike in excess safety violations (jumping from 0.045 for the Oracle baseline to 0.651 for the Prospective critic at Grid 1, T=6).

**Easy regime** (feasibility margin $\in [3, \infty)$): $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ exhibits the opposite failure — premature mask softening. On Grid 2 at $T=15$, $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ begins to decay the safety penalty 7.17 steps earlier than $\mathcal{S}_d$ on average, entering unnecessary constraint-violating behavior in episodes where strict boundary enforcement would complete the mission without incident. Excess violations increase from 0.118 under $\mathcal{S}_d$ to 0.267 under $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ — a 126% increase. This overestimation arises because $\pi_u$'s aggressive behavior during training associates many safe states with high violation probability, teaching $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ to anticipate danger even when the strictly-bounded path is unobstructed.

---

## 5.4 Estimator Misclassification Error (Calibration Analysis)

A critic with compressed scores but correct ordering would still be a valid relaxation trigger with a recalibrated threshold. To rule this out, we evaluate both critics as binary classifiers predicting whether the agent will become strictly-infeasible within $k=5$ steps under strict constraint enforcement $\pi_d$.

| Critic               | AUROC ($k=5$) | 95% CI         |
| -------------------- | ------------- | -------------- |
| $\mathcal{S}_d$      | 0.900         | [0.893, 0.908] |
| $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ | 0.914         | [0.905, 0.922] |

$\mathcal{S}_d$ achieves an AUROC of **0.900**, confirming it reliably distinguishes states that will become infeasible from those that will not. Interestingly, $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ achieves a comparable (and slightly higher) AUROC of **0.914**. This demonstrates that $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ is a highly effective **ranker**—it understands the relative risk of different states. However, CDDP is designed as a continuously-updating online architecture where dynamic thresholds depend on absolute probabilities.

The failure of $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ as a boundary relaxation estimator is revealed by its absolute calibration. While $\mathcal{S}_d$ shows a massive **0.722** gap between "Infeasible" and "Feasible" groups (0.963 vs 0.241), $\mathcal{S}_\theta$'s gap is significantly smaller at **0.543** (0.672 vs 0.129). More importantly, in the critical dilemma zone where the transition to infeasibility is imminent, $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ scores often hover near or below the relaxation threshold $\tau=0.3$, whereas $\mathcal{S}_d$ scores are well above it. $\mathcal{S}_\theta$'s downward bias is not a mere "scaling factor" that can be corrected by adjusting $\tau$, but rather a systematic failure to recognize the absolute magnitude of strict feasibility risk, due to its training on rollouts that allow for constraint violations.

---

## 5.5 Threshold Sweep: The Irreducibility Test

To determine whether the bidirectional miscalibration of $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ is a scaling artifact or a structural failure, we perform an "irreducibility test" by sweeping the relaxation threshold $\tau' \in \{0.05, \dots, 0.30\}$. A correction is only valid if a single $\tau'$ can simultaneously recover the $\mathcal{S}_d$ baseline performance in both the **tight** (delayed relaxation) and **easy** (premature mask softening) regimes.

![Threshold Sweep Results](results/threshold_sweep_v2.png)

As shown in the analysis above, the miscalibration is **irreducible**:

1.  **Tight Regime Fixes Cause Easy Regime Failures:** Lowering the threshold to $\tau' = 0.05$ successfully recovers the mission success rate in the tight regime, bringing $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ within the $\pm 2$pp tolerance band of $\mathcal{S}_d$. However, this same threshold causes $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ to begin to decay the safety penalty almost immediately in easy-regime states, resulting in mean violations of approximately **0.65** — approximately 450% above $\mathcal{S}_d$'s baseline of 0.118.
2.  **Easy Regime Fixes Cause Tight Regime Failures:** Raising the threshold to $\tau' = 0.30$ reduces premature mask softening in the easy regime, but strictly limits the agent's ability to recover in the tight regime, breaking the architecture's capacity to dynamically navigate the mission-feasibility gap.
3.  **No Simultaneous Solution:** No single $\tau'$ exists that brings $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ performance into both tolerance bands simultaneously. The "optimal" trade-off point still leaves $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ significantly under-performing $\mathcal{S}_d$ in at least one regime.

The miscalibration of $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ is not a merely linear shift that can be corrected by threshold tuning. Instead, $\mathcal{S}_\theta$ is **bidirectionally miscalibrated**: it lacks the resolution to distinguish between imminent strict failure and distant safety margins. This failure is a direct consequence of its training distribution; by observing only unconstrained rollouts, the critic fails to learn the sharp, absolute boundary of strict feasibility.

---

## 5.6 Why Training Distribution Determines Calibration

The preceding results establish a structural property of stakes estimation that generalizes beyond the specific critics evaluated here.

**Proposition 4 (Covariate Shift in Stakes Estimation).**
We demonstrate how known principles of covariate shift fatally compromise stakes-based safety architectures when the estimator's training distribution diverges from the monitored policy. Specifically, $\mathcal{S}(s;\,\pi)$ achieves structural alignment for boundary relaxation when — and in practice only when — its training rollouts are generated by the policy being monitored, here $\pi_d$.

*Informal argument.* The CDDP boundary relaxation condition requires $\mathcal{S}(s)$ to approximate $P(\varphi(s_{t'}, T_{\mathrm{rem},t'}) = 0 \mid s_t = s, \pi_d)$ — the probability of future constraint infeasibility **under $\pi_d$**. A critic trained on $\pi_u$ rollouts instead approximates a related but distinct quantity: the probability of mission failure under $\pi_u$, conditioned on the unconstrained policy's ability to violate constraints. Because $\pi_u$ can recover from states that $\pi_d$ cannot — precisely by taking the unsafe shortcut — this quantity is systematically lower in dilemma-zone states, producing the underestimation documented in Section~5.1. The mismatch is not a failure of the critic architecture or training procedure; it is a consequence of approximating the wrong conditional distribution.

This observation has a practical implication for any stakes-based relaxation architecture: the training policy for the stakes estimator must match the policy being monitored at runtime. In CDDP, this means collecting $\mathcal{S}$ training data exclusively under $\pi_d$ execution, even though the deployed system will spend a fraction of episodes with expanded boundaries. Mixing rollout sources would corrupt the calibration in proportion to the $\pi_u$ admixture.

This is a direct instantiation of off-policy evaluation error (Precup et al., 2000; Ross et al., 2011): a critic trained under behavior policy $\pi_u$ cannot reliably estimate value under target policy $\pi_d$ without importance correction, and importance weighting is intractable here because the policies have non-overlapping support in the dilemma zone. The contribution of this section is not to discover this principle but to empirically characterize precisely how it manifests — and proves irreducible — in the specific structure of stakes-based constraint relaxation.
